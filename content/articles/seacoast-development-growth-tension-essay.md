It is worth remembering, amid current debates over short-term rentals and historic district conversions, that Portsmouth's downtown was not always the postcard-ready destination it is today. Decades of deliberate civic investment and private risk-taking transformed a struggling waterfront commercial district into one of New England's most sought-after small cities. That success is now generating its own, less comfortable set of questions.

A downtown popular enough to support boutique hotels in converted historic buildings is, definitionally, a downtown where long-term residential tenants and modest retail are going to struggle to compete for the same square footage. This is not a uniquely Portsmouth problem, and it is not evidence that the city's revival was a mistake. It is simply what success eventually requires a city to confront: deciding, deliberately, who its downtown is ultimately for.

There is no version of this choice that avoids tradeoffs entirely. A city that prioritizes preserving housing and everyday retail downtown will likely grow more slowly and generate less tax revenue from tourism than one that leans fully into its role as a destination. Portsmouth does not have to choose one extreme or the other. But pretending the tension does not exist, or that better design guidelines alone can dissolve it, is its own kind of avoidance. The city earned the right to have this conversation by succeeding. It should have the conversation honestly.
