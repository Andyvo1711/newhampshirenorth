Every March, a peculiar and increasingly rare form of democracy plays out in gymnasiums and town halls across New Hampshire: ordinary residents, not elected representatives, vote line by line on next year's municipal budget. It is slow, sometimes tedious, and occasionally hijacked by a single loud voice. It is also one of the last places in American civic life where this much direct power remains in the hands of the people who actually pay the bill.

Critics of town meeting are not wrong that it has real flaws. Turnout is often a small fraction of registered voters, meaning outcomes can be decided by whoever happens to be free on a Tuesday evening in March. Some towns have moved to official ballot voting for budget articles specifically to broaden participation beyond the deliberative session, a reasonable compromise that preserves debate while widening the vote itself.

## The Alternative Is Worse

But the answer to town meeting's imperfections should be reform, not abandonment. In most of the country, municipal budgets are set entirely by elected councils and professional staff, with public comment as a courtesy rather than a binding mechanism. New Hampshire residents who show up to town meeting are not merely commenting on their tax bill. They are setting it, together, in a room where a road agent or school board member has to explain and defend a line item to the person paying for it.

As the state grows and modernizes, there will be continued pressure to streamline this process into something more efficient and less time-consuming. Efficiency is not nothing. But it is worth remembering what town meeting actually protects: a level of direct civic accountability that most Americans, in most places, no longer have any real equivalent for.
