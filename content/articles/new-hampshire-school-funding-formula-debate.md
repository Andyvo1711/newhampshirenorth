New Hampshire's method for distributing state education aid is under fresh scrutiny after several school districts reported budget shortfalls despite steady or increasing local property tax collections. Superintendents from both rural and suburban districts told lawmakers this month that the current per-pupil formula fails to account for the actual cost of special education services, transportation across sparsely populated towns, and building maintenance in aging schools.

The debate is not new to New Hampshire, where courts have repeatedly weighed in on the adequacy of state education funding over the past three decades. What has changed, district officials say, is the combination of enrollment decline in some communities and enrollment growth in others, creating funding mismatches that the existing formula was never designed to handle smoothly.

## A Divided Legislature

Lawmakers remain split along familiar lines. Some legislators favor a modest increase in the base per-pupil grant paired with targeted supplements for high-need districts. Others argue the state should first commission an updated cost study before committing to any formula change, citing concerns about long-term fiscal sustainability.

School boards in several communities say they are already building contingency budgets in case no resolution is reached before the next town meeting cycle, a sign of how directly this statehouse debate will be felt in individual classrooms this fall.
