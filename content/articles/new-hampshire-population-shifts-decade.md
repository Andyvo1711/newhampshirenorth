New population estimates released this summer confirm a pattern demographers have tracked for years: New Hampshire's growth is increasingly concentrated in its southern tier and along the Seacoast, while several North Country towns have lost population for consecutive census cycles. The divergence carries direct consequences for everything from school enrollment projections to the long-term viability of small-town fire departments staffed largely by volunteers.

Planners note that the state's overall population continues to grow modestly, driven in large part by in-migration rather than natural increase, since deaths have outpaced births statewide for several years running. Most new arrivals are settling within commuting distance of the Massachusetts border or in Seacoast communities with access to coastal amenities and regional job centers.

## What It Means for Small Towns

For towns losing residents, the practical effects are already visible: declining school enrollment has forced several districts to consider consolidating grade levels or sending students to neighboring towns under tuition agreements. Volunteer fire departments in some North Country communities report increasing difficulty recruiting new members as the working-age population shrinks.

State economic development officials say reversing the trend in shrinking towns will likely require a combination of broadband access, workforce housing, and remote-work-friendly infrastructure, rather than any single policy change.
