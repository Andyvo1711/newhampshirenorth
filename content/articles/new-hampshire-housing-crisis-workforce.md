For the third consecutive year, business groups across New Hampshire are naming housing availability, not wages or taxes, as the single greatest constraint on hiring. Manufacturers in Manchester, hospitals in the Upper Valley, and hospitality employers on the Seacoast all report the same pattern: qualified applicants accept offers, then withdraw once they cannot find a place to live within a reasonable commute.

State housing officials estimate New Hampshire needs tens of thousands of additional housing units over the next decade to keep pace with projected job growth, a target that current permitting rates fall well short of meeting. Much of the shortfall is concentrated in starter homes and multi-family units, the categories most directly tied to entry-level and mid-career workers rather than retirees or second-home buyers.

## Towns Weigh Zoning Changes

A growing number of municipalities are reconsidering single-family zoning rules that have historically limited density near town centers and transit corridors. Planning boards in several southern New Hampshire communities have introduced accessory dwelling unit ordinances intended to add housing stock without large new developments, though implementation has been uneven and often contested at town meeting.

Economic development officials argue that without more aggressive local action, the workforce shortage will simply push growth-oriented employers to expand in neighboring states instead of New Hampshire, even when the underlying business case favors staying. Business associations from Nashua to Berlin have begun coordinating public testimony in support of zoning reform bills currently before the legislature, arguing that housing policy has effectively become economic development policy.
