Loggers and land managers across the North Country say sustainable forestry certification, once considered largely a marketing exercise, has become a practical necessity as major buyers increasingly require certified wood for supply chains tied to biomass energy and engineered wood products.

Foresters describe certification as requiring meaningful changes to harvest planning, including longer rotation cycles on some parcels and buffer zones around waterways that reduce the total harvestable timber on a given tract. Landowners who have pursued certification say the process is time-consuming but has opened access to buyers willing to pay a premium for verified sustainable sourcing.

The shift comes as the region's working forests face competing pressures, including conservation easements that permanently limit development while preserving timber harvesting rights, and growing interest from carbon offset programs that pay landowners to defer harvesting altogether on certain parcels. Foresters say navigating these overlapping incentives has become as much a part of the job as the harvesting itself.
