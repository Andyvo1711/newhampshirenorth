Precision manufacturers along Manchester's former mill corridor say they are investing in automated machining equipment at a pace unusual for the region, a shift plant managers describe less as a strategy to cut headcount and more as a necessary response to a persistent shortage of experienced machinists nearing retirement age.

Several shop owners say they simply cannot hire fast enough to replace departing skilled workers, particularly for computer numerical control programming roles that require years of training to perform reliably. New automated systems, while expensive to install, can handle repetitive precision tasks with less specialized oversight, freeing remaining experienced staff to focus on programming, quality control, and more complex custom orders.

## Training Partnerships Expand

Community college partnerships aimed at training the next generation of machinists have expanded significantly over the past two years, with several Manchester-area manufacturers now sponsoring apprenticeship pathways that combine paid on-the-job training with coursework. Industry groups say enrollment has grown, but not yet fast enough to fully offset expected retirements over the next decade.

Manufacturers describe the current moment as a transition rather than a crisis, one where automation and workforce development need to advance together if the region's manufacturing base is to remain competitive against larger out-of-state facilities.
