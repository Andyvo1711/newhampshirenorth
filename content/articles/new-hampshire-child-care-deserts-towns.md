A statewide shortage of licensed child care providers is reshaping household decisions across New Hampshire, with several rural towns now lacking a single full-time infant or toddler program within a reasonable drive. Parents in these communities describe choosing between long waitlists, informal arrangements with relatives, or leaving the workforce altogether until a child reaches school age.

Providers say the underlying economics of child care make the shortage difficult to solve through market forces alone. Staffing ratios required for infant care mean centers can only serve a limited number of children per teacher, capping potential revenue even when demand and pricing would otherwise support expansion. Combined with the same housing and wage pressures affecting other sectors, many centers report difficulty simply retaining qualified staff, let alone growing capacity.

Some employers, particularly hospitals and manufacturers with large shift-based workforces, have begun exploring on-site or employer-sponsored child care as a recruitment tool. Advocates argue that without broader state investment in the sector, individual employer solutions will only reach a small fraction of families affected by the shortage.
